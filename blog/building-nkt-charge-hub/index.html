What Building an EV Charging Station Actually Taught Me

NKT Charge Hub has been operating in Thodupuzha since March 2026. This is what went into it — the site, the specification, and the three unglamorous things that decide whether a charger is a business or a prop.

26 October 2026 · 5 min read · By George S. Thomas

A charging point looks like a piece of furniture until you own one. Then it becomes three businesses wearing one cabinet: an energy business, a software business, and a service business that has to answer a phone when something does not work.

Why charging

I wanted a business that could not be faked. Software can look finished while it is held together with hope; a charger either delivers power to a car or it does not, and the driver knows within ninety seconds which one happened.

The second reason is boring and more honest: electric vehicles are arriving in Kerala whether or not anyone in my position builds for them. Charging is the part of that shift that behaves like real estate — a location, a connection, a long asset life — and it sits close enough to the technology work I already do that the two can feed each other.

Choosing the site

NKT Charge Hub operates at Nedumpurath Towers in Thodupuzha, a property already inside the NKT ecosystem. That decision was not clever, it was structural: when the site is an asset you already hold and understand, the questions you are left with are the ones that matter — power, access, visibility, and whether a driver can actually manoeuvre into the bay.

Location in charging is not about being central. It is about being on the way: on a route people already drive, with enough space to wait, and enough visibility that a stranger trusts it in the dark.

The specification, and why it matters

The site runs 90 kW of DC fast charging through dual-gun CCS2 connectors, with OCPP-compatible charging management behind it. Three choices, three reasons:

  • DC, not AC. Fast charging is the product. Slow charging is a amenity you offer while someone shops for two hours — a different business with a different customer.
  • CCS2. It is the connector the Indian market has settled on for DC charging. Anything else is a bet that the country will change its mind.
  • OCPP-compatible management. Standards-based control means the site is not locked to one vendor's software. If the network around it changes, the charger can change with it instead of becoming expensive furniture.

“A charging point is not a plug. It is an energy system, a software system and a service business wearing one cabinet.”

Three things nobody puts in the brochure

Uptime is the product

Drivers do not buy kilowatt-hours; they buy the confidence that the next charger will work. A site that is down at 9pm on a Sunday costs more than the session it lost — it teaches that driver to plan around you, and they will keep that habit. Availability is the entire reputation of the business, and it is built out of maintenance, spares, and someone actually looking at the alerts.

Load, demand and the grid

The electrical reality sits underneath everything: sanctioned load, how demand behaves across a day, what happens when two vehicles want power at once. This is the part of EV infrastructure that marketing never mentions, and the part you cannot fake your way through. Understanding your own load curve is what makes every later decision — pricing, expansion, storage — something other than a guess.

Monitoring is not optional

Digital monitoring and CCTV started as security and turned into operations. You cannot manage what you cannot see: which sessions failed, what the fault codes say, whether a fault cleared itself or is waiting for a human. Remote visibility is the difference between hearing about a problem from a customer and fixing it before they arrive.

Where the technology work comes in

This is the seam between the business and THOMU LAB. Charging generates exactly the kind of operational data that is useless in a spreadsheet and valuable in a system: session histories, faults, utilisation patterns, energy delivered, downtime reasons.

The work in the lab — automation, reporting, and eventually the intelligence layer I am building as My_AI_OS — exists to turn that stream into something I can act on without spending my evening reading logs. It is not a science project bolted onto a business; it is the reason the business can be run by a small number of people.

What I am deliberately not publishing

No utilisation figures, no revenue, no energy numbers, no pricing, no expansion dates. Not because they are secret in any dramatic sense, but because publishing early numbers invites people to judge a twelve-month-old asset by the standards of a mature network. When there is something worth reporting, it will be reported with the date attached — and this page will say so rather than quietly rounding up.

What comes next

Three directions, in order: make monitoring genuinely useful rather than merely present; automate the reporting so the numbers arrive without being asked for; and learn enough from one site to know exactly what a second one would need. The last thing this business needs is a second location before the first one is boring.

The wider interest stays where it started — vehicle technology, energy storage, and intelligent mobility. Charging is the entry point, not the whole map.
